# Spiced red cabbage in red wine vinegar (Ball)

This card describes the Ball recipe for spiced red cabbage, as reprinted by University of California Master Food Preservers: shredded red cabbage salted, rinsed and dried, packed into hot jars, covered with hot red wine vinegar spiced with cloves and other whole spices, and processed in a boiling-water bath. [T:ACI-876,ACI-877,ACI-878]

## !

- Do not change the proportions of vinegar and cabbage and do not use vinegar of unknown acidity; use only recipes with tested proportions. [T:ACI-016]
- Use red wine vinegar here only because this tested Ball recipe calls for it: according to UCCE Mariposa, specialty vinegars such as red or white wine vinegar are used only when a research-tested recipe specifies them. [T:ACI-702,ACI-876]

## Why you must not change the recipe

Cabbage is not an acid food on its own: the FDA list gives cabbage a pH of about 5.20–6.80, while acid foods are foods with a natural pH of 4.6 or lower. [T:ACI-082,ACI-001]

The whole product must contain a minimum, uniform level of acid so that botulism bacteria cannot grow. [T:ACI-017]

Extension and USDA have no recipe for canning red cabbage; Ask Extension points to a tested Spiced Red Cabbage recipe from Ball. [T:ACI-875]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): this card covers only this Ball recipe and only red cabbage, not savoy cabbage or other cabbage pickles with other amounts or extra steps. [T:ACI-875,ACI-876]

Do not change the amount of vinegar or cabbage and do not add other vegetables. [T:ACI-016]

NCHFP generally recommends white distilled and cider vinegars of 5% acidity for pickles; this Ball recipe calls for red wine vinegar of 5% acidity instead. [T:ACI-018,ACI-876]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): use only red wine vinegar labelled 5% acidity; do not use vinegar of unknown acidity, such as homemade vinegar or vinegar without an acidity label. [T:ACI-876,ACI-016]

## Ingredients

A US cup holds about 237 ml. [T:ACI-195]

According to Ball, the recipe uses 12 pounds (about 5.4 kg) of red cabbage (3–4 heads), cored and shredded, and 1/2 cup of canning or pickling salt. [T:ACI-876]

For the pickling liquid: 8 cups (2 quarts) of red wine vinegar of 5% acidity, 1 cup of light brown sugar, 1/2 cup of mustard seed and 1/4 cup of mace or nutmeg. [T:ACI-876]

For the spice bag: 1/4 cup each of whole cloves, whole allspice, whole peppercorns and celery seed, and 2 cinnamon sticks. [T:ACI-876]

According to the UCCE handout, the recipe makes about 5 quarts or 10 pints. [T:ACI-876]

## Salting and drying the cabbage

In a large crock or a glass or stainless steel bowl, layer the cabbage with the salt, cover and let it stand in a cool place for 24 hours. [T:ACI-877]

Drain the cabbage in a colander, rinse it with cool running water and leave it in the colander for 1 hour to drain. [T:ACI-877]

Spread it on trays lined with paper towels and let it dry for about 6 hours. [T:ACI-877]

## Pickling liquid

Tie the cloves, allspice, peppercorns, celery seed and cinnamon in a spice bag. [T:ACI-876]

Bring the vinegar, sugar, mustard seed, mace and spice bag to a boil and boil gently for 5 minutes, then remove the spice bag. [T:ACI-877]

## Filling and processing

JarAtlas rule (cautious): process only pint jars, because the handout’s times do not say which jar size they are for. [T:ACI-878]

Pack the cabbage into hot jars and ladle the hot pickling liquid over it to cover, leaving 1/2 inch (about 1.3 cm) of headspace. [T:ACI-877]

Remove air bubbles and, if needed, add more of the same spiced vinegar to keep the headspace; never top up with water. [T:ACI-877,ACI-016]

Close the jars with two-piece canning lids and process them in a boiling-water bath. [T:SLO-223,ACI-878]

According to the UCCE handout, up to 1000 ft (up to 300 m) process the jars for 20 minutes. [T:ACI-878,ACI-176]

Above 1000 ft the food may spoil if the time is not increased. [T:ACI-015]

Above 1000 ft (above 300 m) process them for 25 minutes, and above 6000 ft (above 1800 m) for 30 minutes. [T:ACI-878,ACI-176]

The Ball version quoted by Ask Extension gives 20 minutes without altitude times; the UCCE handout also has a second, shorter set of times below the recipe that may belong to another recipe, so JarAtlas uses the longer times above. [T:ACI-878]

The card converts feet to metres and rounds the limits down, towards the longer time: 1000 ft becomes 300 m and 6000 ft becomes 1800 m. [T:ACI-176]

Shortening the time or cooling jars in cold water can also lead to spoilage. [T:ACI-015]

The UCCE handout gives the yield in quarts or pints, but its times do not say which jar size they are for. [T:ACI-878]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): use only pint jars, of up to 473 ml, because the times are not given for a particular jar size; do not use quart or 1 l jars for this recipe. [T:ACI-878,ACI-174]

## Cooling and storing

When you take the hot jars out of the canner, do not retighten the lids or bands; this can cut through the gasket and cause seal failures. [T:SLO-210]

Cool the jars at room temperature for 12 to 24 hours, then remove only the screw bands (the metal rings of two-piece lids), leaving the flat lids on the jars, and test the seal: press the middle of the lid, and if it springs up when you release it, the jar is not sealed. [T:SLO-196,SLO-212]

NCHFP calls Mason-type canning jars with self-sealing two-piece lids (a flat lid and a screw band) the best choice, and this seal test and the method on this card assume them; NCHFP does not recommend other commercial jars whose mouths cannot be sealed with two-piece canning lids for canning any food at home. [T:SLO-223,SLO-212]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): use this card only with jars that take two-piece canning lids; it gives no method for twist-off jars or jars with glass lids, so do not use them for this recipe. [T:SLO-223]

A sealed lid is concave, curved down slightly in the centre; if the centre is flat or bulging, the jar may not be sealed. [T:SLO-196]

If a jar has not sealed at this check, keep it in the refrigerator and eat it within several days. [T:SLO-197]

Store sealed jars in a clean, cool, dark, dry place, not above 95 °F (35 °C) and not near hot pipes, a stove or a furnace, in an uninsulated attic or in direct sunlight; in such conditions the food may spoil. [T:SLO-198]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): keep an opened jar in the refrigerator and use it within 1 week. [T:SLO-322]

## When to throw it out

Before using a stored jar, check that the lid is tight and holds a vacuum: a good seal has a concave centre. [T:SLO-191]

Look for streaks of dried food running from the top of the jar, rising air bubbles and unnatural colour. [T:SLO-191]

When opening the jar, check for an unnatural smell, spurting liquid and cotton-like mould on the food or under the lid. [T:SLO-191]

Do not taste food from a jar with an unsealed lid or with signs of spoilage. [T:SLO-190]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): throw out such a jar. [T:SLO-190]

NCHFP warns that spoiled low-acid foods, including tomatoes, may show different signs of spoilage or very few, so every suspect jar of such food should be treated as if it had produced botulinum toxin and handled carefully. [T:SLO-350]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): handle every suspect jar from this card this way, because its main ingredient is low in acid or close to the acidity limit. [T:SLO-350]

If a suspect jar is still sealed, put it in a heavy garbage bag, close the bag and put it in a regular trash container. [T:SLO-350]

If it is unsealed, open or leaking, NCHFP says it should be detoxified before disposal; JarAtlas does not repeat that procedure here, so follow the detoxification and clean-up steps on the NCHFP page “Identifying and Handling Spoiled Canned Food” (nchfp.uga.edu/how/can/general-information/identifying-and-handling-spoiled-canned-food/) and avoid contact with suspect food or liquid. [T:SLO-350]

Contact with botulinum toxin can be fatal if it is swallowed or enters through any openings in the skin, so wear rubber or heavy plastic gloves when handling suspect jars and cleaning up. [T:SLO-350]

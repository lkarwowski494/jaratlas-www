# Blackberry vinegar with a rosemary sprig (UGA and OSU method)

This card uses the general UGA and OSU method for fruit vinegars with blackberries, which OSU lists among especially flavorful fruits for flavored vinegars. [T:NAP-044,NAP-073,NAP-084]

## Ingredients

Per pint (2 cups) of vinegar use 1 to 2 cups of blackberries, and not more. [T:NAP-073,NAP-084]

Apple cider vinegar blends best with fruits, according to UGA. [T:NAP-081]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): neither source gives an amount of rosemary to steep together with fruit, so steep only the blackberries and add one washed and sanitized sprig of rosemary to the bottle at the end, as UGA allows. [T:NAP-077,NAP-036]

For added safety, use only commercially produced vinegar. [T:NAP-037]

Wine and rice vinegars contain some protein that is an excellent medium for bacterial growth if they are not handled and stored properly. [T:NAP-037]

Vinegar with less than 5% acidity should never be used in home food preservation. [T:ACI-516]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): do not replace it with a stronger vinegar and do not dilute a stronger vinegar with water yourself; Penn State Extension advises staying within 5–7% acidity for home food preservation and diluting vinegar only when a tested recipe says so. [T:ACI-209,ACI-210]

Do not add more flavoring than this card gives: too much plant material can destroy the acid and ruin the preservative quality of the vinegar. [T:NAP-036]

A US pint holds about 473 ml and a quart about 946 ml. [T:ACI-174]

## Preparing

Wash glass jars or bottles in warm, soapy water, then sterilize them by immersing them completely in boiling water for 10 minutes; fill them with vinegar while they are still warm. [T:NAP-070]

Close them with non-corrodible metal or plastic screw caps, washed in hot soapy water, rinsed and scalded in boiling water, or with new, pre-sterilized corks dipped in and out of boiling water with tongs 3–4 times. [T:NAP-071]

Work in a very clean area with clean utensils and very clean hands. [T:NAP-086]

Wash the blackberries thoroughly with clean water; they may be left whole or halved. [T:NAP-073]

Place them in the sterilized jar without overpacking it. [T:NAP-073]

Heat the vinegar to just below the boiling point, to at least 190–195 °F (88–91 °C). [T:NAP-038]

Pour it over the flavoring in the jar, leaving 1/4 inch of headspace, wipe the rims with a clean, damp cloth, close the jars tightly and let them cool undisturbed. [T:NAP-075]

Let the jar stand in a cool, dark place until the flavor is right, usually 2–4 weeks. [T:NAP-084]

Strain the vinegar through damp cheesecloth or a coffee filter, one or more times, until it shows no cloudiness, and discard the solids. [T:NAP-076]

Wash the rosemary sprig gently but thoroughly and pat it dry with a clean paper towel. [T:NAP-072]

Then dip it in a sanitizing solution of 1 teaspoon of household chlorine bleach in 6 cups of water, rinse it thoroughly under cold water and pat it dry, as UGA directs. [T:NAP-072]

Pour the strained vinegar into clean, sterilized jars, add the rosemary sprig and cap them tightly. [T:NAP-077]

Label each bottle with the base vinegar, the flavoring and the date. [T:NAP-085]

## Storing

Keep the vinegar in the refrigerator: some harmful bacteria may survive and even multiply slowly in some vinegars. [T:NAP-040]

Fruit vinegars may start to brown and change flavor noticeably after 3 months in cool storage. [T:NAP-078]

For best quality, use it within 4–6 months, as OSU Extension advises for flavored vinegars. [T:NAP-065]

Date the bottle when you open it. [T:NAP-078]

Bottles kept as decorations on sunny window sills or shelves for more than a few weeks should be treated as decorations and not used in food. [T:NAP-079]

## When to throw it away

If the vinegar shows mold or signs of fermentation such as bubbling, cloudiness or sliminess, throw it away without tasting it. [T:NAP-041]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): do not use this fruit vinegar for making pickles; OSU allows flavored herb vinegar for pickle making only when it has a 5% acidity level. [T:NAP-042]

# Raspberry vinegar steeped in store-bought vinegar (UGA)

This card follows the tested University of Georgia (UGA) raspberry vinegar recipe: raspberries steeped in commercially produced vinegar, strained and kept in the refrigerator. [T:NAP-043,NAP-080]

## Ingredients

For one quart jar: 2 cups fresh raspberries and 3 cups vinegar. [T:NAP-080]

Apple cider vinegar blends best with fruits, according to UGA. [T:NAP-081]

Use only commercially produced vinegar: wine and rice vinegars contain some protein that is an excellent medium for bacterial growth. [T:NAP-037]

Vinegar with less than 5% acidity should never be used in home food preservation. [T:ACI-516]

Do not replace it with a stronger vinegar and do not dilute a stronger vinegar with water yourself; Penn State Extension advises staying within 5–7% acidity for home food preservation and diluting vinegar only when a tested recipe says so. [T:ACI-209,ACI-210]

Do not add more flavoring than this card gives: too much plant material can destroy the acid and ruin the preservative quality of the vinegar. [T:NAP-036]

A US pint holds about 473 ml and a quart about 946 ml. [T:ACI-174]

## Preparing

Wash glass jars or bottles in warm, soapy water, then sterilize them by immersing them completely in boiling water for 10 minutes; fill them with vinegar while they are still warm. [T:NAP-070]

Close them with non-corrodible metal or plastic screw caps, or with new, pre-sterilized corks. [T:NAP-071]

Wash the raspberries gently but thoroughly and bruise them slightly with the back of a spoon. [T:NAP-080]

Place them in a sterilized quart glass canning jar. [T:NAP-080]

Heat the vinegar to just below the boiling point, to at least 190–195 °F (88–91 °C), and pour it over the raspberries. [T:NAP-038,NAP-080]

Cap the jar tightly and let it stand for 2 to 3 weeks in a cool, dark place. [T:NAP-080]

Strain the vinegar through damp cheesecloth and discard the fruit; if it is still cloudy, strain it again, for example through a coffee filter. [T:NAP-080,NAP-076]

Pour the vinegar into clean, sterilized glass jars or bottles and seal them tightly. [T:NAP-080]

Label each bottle with the base vinegar, the flavoring and the date. [T:NAP-085]

## Storing

Store the vinegar in the refrigerator for best quality and flavor. [T:NAP-080]

Keep the vinegar in the refrigerator: some harmful bacteria may survive and even multiply slowly in some vinegars. [T:NAP-040]

Fruit vinegars may start to brown and change flavor noticeably after 3 months in cool storage. [T:NAP-078]

For best quality, use it within 4–6 months, as OSU Extension advises for refrigerated flavored vinegars. [T:NAP-065]

Date the bottle when you open it. [T:NAP-078]

Bottles kept as decorations on sunny window sills or shelves for more than a few weeks should be treated as decorations and not used in food. [T:NAP-079]

## When to throw it away

If the vinegar shows mold or signs of fermentation such as bubbling, cloudiness or sliminess, throw it away without tasting it. [T:NAP-041]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): OSU says flavored herb vinegar should not be used for making pickles unless it has a 5% acidity level, and JarAtlas applies the same rule to this fruit vinegar. [T:NAP-042]

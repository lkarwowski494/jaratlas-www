# Dried parsley leaves (NCHFP)

This card describes how to dry parsley leaves: in small bundles hung indoors, or in a food dehydrator. [T:SUS-081,SUS-031]

## Choosing and preparing

Herbs (leaves, flowers or seeds) are dried by exposing them to warm, dry air in a well-ventilated place until the moisture evaporates. [T:SUS-076]

Most herbs are best harvested for drying just before the first flowers open, early in the morning after the dew has dried. [T:SUS-078]

After harvest, do not leave the herbs in the sun or unattended, and avoid bruising the leaves. [T:SUS-077]

Rinse the herbs in cool water, gently shake off excess water and discard bruised, soiled or damaged leaves and stems. [T:SUS-077]

## Drying

Parsley is one of the sturdier herbs (with rosemary, sage, thyme and summer savory) that are the easiest to dry without a dehydrator: tie it in small bundles and hang them to dry. [T:SUS-081]

Dry the bundles indoors: they usually keep better colour and flavour than outdoors. [T:SUS-081]

Do not dry herbs in the sun: they can lose flavour and colour. [T:SUS-032]

In a dehydrator, dry herbs at 95–115 °F (about 35–46 °C) in a single layer on the trays; in humid areas up to 125 °F (about 52 °C) may be needed, and drying takes 1–4 hours. [T:SUS-031]

Do not use trays or screens made of galvanized mesh; also avoid copper and aluminium mesh. [T:SUS-008]

Herbs are dry when they crumble and the stems break when bent; leaves ready for packing are crisp and crumble easily between your fingers. [T:SUS-079]

## Packing and storing

Pack only completely cooled dried food: warm dried food sweats, and that moisture can be enough for mould to grow. [T:SUS-024]

Dried food is susceptible to insects and moisture reabsorption, so once completely cool, pack it at once into clean, dry insect-proof containers, as tightly as possible without crushing. [T:SUS-205]

Store dried food in clean, dry canning jars, plastic freezer containers with tight-fitting lids or plastic freezer bags. [T:SUS-064]

Packaging must be food grade: do not use trash bags or plastic or fibreboard containers that previously held non-food items. [T:SUS-065]

Pack amounts that can be used at one time: each opening exposes the dried food to air and moisture, which lowers quality and can lead to spoilage. [T:SUS-067]

Vacuum packaging is not a preservation method by itself: removing oxygen increases the risk of botulism, and a product that needs refrigeration still needs it. [T:SUS-025]

Store dried food in a cool, dry, dark place. [T:SUS-066]

Do not keep dried herbs in oil at room temperature: oil keeps out oxygen, and spores of Clostridium botulinum may already be present in garlic and herbs; without oxygen, with low acid and at room temperature they can produce a deadly toxin. [T:SLO-321]

Drying does not make this safe: according to UGA, dried garlic and herbs may still not be dry enough to give a safe product. [T:SLO-321]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): if you put dried herbs in oil, keep the oil in the refrigerator and use it within 4 days of making it, or freeze it. [T:SLO-321]

Airtight containers and a cool, dry, dark place protect the colour and fragrance of the herbs. [T:SUS-080]

Dried herbs are usually 3–4 times stronger than fresh; use 1/4–1/3 of the amount of fresh herbs in a recipe. [T:SUS-084]

## When to throw away

Check dried food often (glass shows condensation); if it has taken up moisture but is not spoiled, use it at once or dry it again and repack it. [T:SUS-023]

Throw away mouldy dried food; also throw away any that smells off. [T:SUS-022]

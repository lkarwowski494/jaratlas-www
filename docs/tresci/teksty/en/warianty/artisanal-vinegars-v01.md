# Homemade cider vinegar, filtered and pasteurized (Penn State Extension)

This card describes how to make cider vinegar at home following Penn State Extension (from Ohio State University Extension): cider from ripe apples ferments into alcohol and then into vinegar, which is filtered and pasteurized. [T:NAP-090,NAP-093,NAP-034]

## !

- Do not use homemade vinegar for pickling: its acidity is not consistent. [T:NAP-029]
- Do not use homemade vinegar in foods to be canned or stored at room temperature; it is good for salads, cooking and refrigerator or freezer pickles. [T:NAP-030]

## What this card covers

This variant is named after the mother of vinegar, but the tested method removes the mother by filtering before storage; this card gives no method for keeping vinegar with its mother. [T:NAP-093,NAP-094]

Mother of vinegar forms while the cider ferments; in this method the finished vinegar is filtered to remove the mother, which prevents further fermentation or spoilage. [T:NAP-032,NAP-093]

This card does not describe keeping vinegar unfiltered with its mother; do not leave out the filtering or the pasteurizing. [T:NAP-093,NAP-094]

Penn State says unfiltered vinegar with a mother is safe for pickling only when it is commercially made, contains at least 5% acid, has been stored properly and is used before its best-before date; homemade vinegar does not meet these conditions. [T:NAP-031,NAP-029]

## Equipment

Do not use metal containers for making or storing vinegar: the acid corrodes metal and aluminium; use glass, plastic, wood, enamel or stainless steel. [T:NAP-035]

Use cheesecloth in place of a lid while the cider ferments, so that air can reach it. [T:NAP-092]

You need a cooking thermometer for pasteurizing. [T:NAP-094]

## Making the cider

Use fall and winter apple varieties; summer and green apples do not contain enough sugar. [T:NAP-090]

Gather the apples, wash them well, crush them into pulp and strain off the juice with a press or cheesecloth. [T:NAP-090]

Adding yeast is not essential, but it speeds up fermentation; use a special cultivated yeast from a wine-making shop, not bread yeast. [T:NAP-091]

## Fermenting

All of the sugar must change into alcohol and then all of the alcohol into acetic acid. [T:NAP-090]

Pour the cider into one or more containers to about three-quarters full and do not close them with lids. [T:NAP-092]

Stir daily and keep the containers away from direct sunlight at 60–80 °F (16–27 °C). [T:NAP-092,NAP-032]

At lower temperatures you do not always get usable vinegar, and higher temperatures interfere with the formation of the mother of vinegar. [T:NAP-032]

Full fermentation takes about 3–4 weeks. [T:NAP-033]

Near the end you should notice a vinegar-like smell; taste samples daily until the vinegar is as strong as you want. [T:NAP-092]

## Filtering and pasteurizing

When the vinegar is fully fermented, filter it through several layers of fine cheesecloth or filter paper; a coffee filter works well. [T:NAP-093]

Heat the vinegar before pouring it into sterilized bottles, then place the bottles in a hot water bath. [T:NAP-094]

The vinegar must reach at least 140 °F (60 °C) and should not go above 160 °F (71 °C); check it with a cooking thermometer. [T:NAP-034,NAP-094]

## Storing

Cap the containers, let them cool and store them at room temperature out of direct sunlight. [T:NAP-094]

Penn State says that pasteurized vinegar stays in excellent condition almost indefinitely. [T:NAP-094]

Use it only for salads, cooking and refrigerator or freezer pickles, never for canning or for foods stored at room temperature. [T:NAP-030]

## When to throw away

Oregon State University Extension says that flavored vinegar that moulds or shows signs of fermentation, such as bubbling, cloudiness or sliminess, should be thrown away without tasting; JarAtlas rule (cautious): do the same with this vinegar. [T:NAP-041]

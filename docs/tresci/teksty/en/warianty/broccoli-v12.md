# Frozen broccoli (NCHFP)

This card describes the NCHFP instructions for freezing broccoli: florets blanched in boiling water or steam, cooled, packed and frozen at 0 °F (−18 °C) or lower. [T:SLO-242,SUS-050]

## Choosing and preparing

Choose firm, young, tender stalks with compact heads. [T:SLO-242]

Remove the leaves and woody parts and separate the heads into pieces of a convenient size. [T:SLO-242]

To remove insects, soak the pieces for 30 minutes in brine made with 4 teaspoons of salt per gallon (about 3.8 l) of water. [T:SLO-242]

Split the pieces lengthwise so that the florets are no more than 1 1/2 inches (about 3.8 cm) across. [T:SLO-242]

## Blanching

Blanching (scalding vegetables in boiling water or steam for a short time) is a must for almost all vegetables you freeze: it stops enzyme action. [T:SUS-053]

Use 1 gallon (about 3.8 l) of water per pound (about 0.45 kg) of vegetables; put the vegetables in a blanching basket, lower it into vigorously boiling water and cover the pot. [T:SUS-055,SLO-254]

The water should return to a boil within 1 minute; start counting the blanching time as soon as it boils again, and keep the heat high for the whole time. [T:SUS-055,SLO-254]

Blanch broccoli for 3 minutes in boiling water, or for 5 minutes in steam. [T:SLO-242]

NCHFP calls both steaming and boiling satisfactory for broccoli; steam blanching takes about 1 1/2 times longer than water blanching. [T:SLO-254]

To steam, use a pot with a tight lid and a basket at least 3 inches (about 7.6 cm) above the bottom, bring an inch or two (about 2.5–5 cm) of water to a boil, put the broccoli in the basket in a single layer, cover and start counting the time as soon as the lid is on. [T:SLO-254]

Do not shorten the time: underblanching stimulates enzyme activity and is worse than no blanching. [T:SUS-054]

NCHFP warns that blanching in a microwave oven may not be effective, because some enzymes may not be inactivated. [T:SUS-057]

Cool the vegetables at once: plunge the basket into a large amount of cold water at 60 °F (about 16 °C) or below, changing the water often or using cold running water or ice water (about 1 pound of ice per pound of vegetables). [T:SUS-056,SLO-254]

Cool them for as long as you blanched them, then drain them thoroughly: extra water lowers the quality of frozen vegetables. [T:SUS-056,SLO-254]

## Packing

Package the drained broccoli leaving no headspace: NCHFP lists loose-packed vegetables such as broccoli among foods that need none. [T:SLO-242,SLO-250]

Use packaging that resists moisture and vapour, is durable and leakproof and does not become brittle and crack in the cold. [T:SLO-251]

Pack the food tightly, leaving as little air as possible, and press as much air as you can out of freezer bags before closing them. [T:SLO-250,SLO-251]

Do not freeze fruits or vegetables in containers holding more than 1/2 gallon (about 1.9 l): food in larger containers freezes too slowly. [T:SLO-251]

Ordinary glass jars break easily at freezer temperatures; if you use glass, choose wide-mouth jars made for both freezing and canning. [T:SLO-251]

In standard narrow-mouth canning jars leave extra headspace, because expanding food can break the jar at the neck. [T:SLO-251]

Close rigid containers with a tight lid and keep the sealing edge clean and dry. [T:SLO-250]

Label each package with the name of the product, any added ingredients, the packaging date, the amount and the form of the food, such as whole or sliced. [T:SLO-250]

## Freezing and storing

Freeze the food at 0 °F (−18 °C) or lower. [T:SUS-050]

For faster freezing, set the freezer to −10 °F (about −23 °C) or lower about 24 hours in advance. [T:SUS-051]

Do not overload the freezer: add only as much unfrozen food as will freeze within 24 hours. [T:SUS-052]

Keep the freezer at 0 °F (−18 °C) or lower, because at higher temperatures food loses quality faster; keep a thermometer in the freezer and check it often. [T:SLO-255]

At 0 °F (−18 °C) fruits and vegetables keep their quality for about 8 to 12 months; after that they should still be safe, only lower in quality. [T:SUS-058]

For the best quality choose the shorter time and use the oldest packages first. [T:SLO-261]

## Thawing and cooking

Most frozen vegetables should be cooked without thawing first. [T:SLO-257]

Bring water to a boil in a covered pan (for most vegetables 1/2 cup of water is enough for a pint package), add the frozen vegetables, cover and bring the water quickly back to a boil. [T:SLO-257]

Then lower the heat and cook them covered until just tender. [T:SLO-257]

Never thaw food at room temperature or in warm water: its outer layer warms into the danger zone of 40–140 °F (about 4–60 °C), where bacteria multiply rapidly. [T:SLO-258,SUS-060]

Thaw it in the refrigerator at 40 °F (about 4 °C) or below, in cold running water below 70 °F (about 21 °C), or in the microwave oven if you will cook or serve it straight away. [T:SLO-258]

## When to throw away

Food is safe while it stays frozen, but once it thaws and rises above 40 °F (about 4 °C), bacteria that were present before freezing can begin to multiply. [T:SUS-059]

During a power cut keep the freezer door closed: according to the FDA, a full freezer keeps its temperature for about 48 hours, or 24 hours if it is half full. [T:SLO-259]

When the power comes back, the FDA says that food which still contains ice crystals or is at 40 °F (about 4 °C) or below is safe to refreeze or cook; you cannot judge this by look or smell. [T:SLO-259]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): throw away a package that has no ice crystals and is above 40 °F (about 4 °C), or that you cannot check. [T:SLO-260]

The FDA also says to discard perishable food that has been at room temperature for 2 hours or more, or 1 hour if it is above 90 °F (about 32 °C) outdoors. [T:SLO-260]

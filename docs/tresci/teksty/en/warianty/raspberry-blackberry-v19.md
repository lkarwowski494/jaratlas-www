# Frozen blackberries (NCHFP)

This card describes the NCHFP instructions for freezing blackberries in syrup, with sugar or without additions, at 0 °F (−18 °C) or lower; the instructions do not include blanching. [T:SLO-265,SUS-050]

## Choosing and preparing

Choose fully ripe, firm berries. [T:SLO-265]

Wash them carefully in cold water, discarding soft, under-ripe or defective fruit. [T:SLO-265]

NCHFP uses US measures: a cup holds about 237 ml. [T:ACI-195]

## Ways of packing

In syrup (syrup pack): put the berries into containers and cover them with cold 40 to 50 percent syrup, depending on how sweet the fruit is. [T:SLO-265]

For a 40 percent (heavy) syrup NCHFP dissolves 2 3/4 cups of sugar in 4 cups of lukewarm water until the syrup is clear, which gives about 5 1/3 cups; chill the syrup before use. [T:SLO-253]

For a 50 percent (very heavy) syrup NCHFP uses 4 cups of sugar to 4 cups of water, which gives about 6 cups. [T:SLO-269]

Use just enough cold syrup to cover the fruit, about 1/2 to 2/3 cup per pint; to keep the fruit under the syrup, put a crumpled piece of parchment paper on top and press the fruit down before closing. [T:SLO-253]

With sugar (sugar pack): gently mix 3/4 cup of sugar with 1 quart (1 1/3 pounds, about 0.6 kg) of berries and fill the containers. [T:SLO-265]

Without additions (dry pack): put the berries into containers, or freeze them first on a tray and pack them as soon as they are frozen. [T:SLO-265]

NCHFP calls the dry pack good for small whole fruits such as berries, which give a good product without sugar. [T:SLO-252]

For a tray pack, spread a single layer of fruit on shallow trays and freeze it; as soon as it is frozen, package it and return it to the freezer, to prevent freezer burn. [T:SLO-252]

Tray-packed fruit stays loose. [T:SLO-252]

## Packing

Fruit packed without added sugar or liquid (dry pack) needs 1/2 inch (about 1.3 cm) of headspace in pint and quart containers, with wide or narrow mouths. [T:SLO-249]

Sugar and syrup packs count as liquid packs: in wide-mouth containers leave 1/2 inch (about 1.3 cm) of headspace for a pint (about 0.47 l) and 1 inch (about 2.5 cm) for a quart (about 0.95 l); in narrow-mouth containers leave 3/4 inch (about 1.9 cm) for a pint and 1 1/2 inches (about 3.8 cm) for a quart. [T:SLO-249]

Use packaging that resists moisture and vapour, is durable and leakproof and does not become brittle and crack in the cold. [T:SLO-251]

Pack the food tightly, leaving as little air as possible, and press as much air as you can out of freezer bags before closing them. [T:SLO-250,SLO-251]

Do not freeze fruits or vegetables in containers holding more than 1/2 gallon (about 1.9 l): food in larger containers freezes too slowly. [T:SLO-251]

Ordinary glass jars break easily at freezer temperatures; if you use glass, choose wide-mouth jars made for both freezing and canning. [T:SLO-251]

In standard narrow-mouth canning jars leave extra headspace, because expanding food can break the jar at the neck. [T:SLO-251]

Close rigid containers with a tight lid and keep the sealing edge clean and dry. [T:SLO-250]

Label each package with the name of the product, any added ingredients, the packaging date, the amount and the form of the food, such as whole or sliced. [T:SLO-250]

JarAtlas rule (cautious; NCHFP gives no headspace for metric containers): treat a container larger than a pint (about 0.47 l) as a quart, and for this fruit do not use containers larger than a quart (about 0.95 l; a 1 l container is already larger), even though the general limit above is 1/2 gallon, because NCHFP gives no headspace for them. [T:SLO-249]

## Freezing and storing

Freeze the food at 0 °F (−18 °C) or lower. [T:SUS-050]

For faster freezing, set the freezer to −10 °F (about −23 °C) or lower about 24 hours in advance. [T:SUS-051]

Do not overload the freezer: add only as much unfrozen food as will freeze within 24 hours. [T:SUS-052]

Keep the freezer at 0 °F (−18 °C) or lower, because at higher temperatures food loses quality faster; keep a thermometer in the freezer and check it often. [T:SLO-255]

At 0 °F (−18 °C) fruits and vegetables keep their quality for about 8 to 12 months; after that they should still be safe, only lower in quality. [T:SUS-058]

For the best quality choose the shorter time and use the oldest packages first. [T:SLO-261]

## Thawing and using

Serve frozen fruit while it still has a few ice crystals: it is then less mushy. [T:SLO-256]

Thaw the fruit in its package in the refrigerator, under running water, or in the microwave oven if you thaw it just before use. [T:SLO-256]

A 1 pound (about 0.45 kg) package of fruit in syrup needs 6 to 8 hours in the refrigerator or 1/2 to 1 hour in cool running water; fruit packed in dry sugar thaws a little faster than fruit in syrup, and both thaw faster than fruit packed without sugar. [T:SLO-256]

Thaw only as much as you need at one time; leftover thawed fruit keeps better if you cook it. [T:SLO-256]

Never thaw food at room temperature or in warm water: its outer layer warms into the danger zone of 40–140 °F (about 4–60 °C), where bacteria multiply rapidly. [T:SLO-258]

Thaw it in the refrigerator at 40 °F (about 4 °C) or below, in cold running water below 70 °F (about 21 °C), or in the microwave oven if you will cook or serve it straight away. [T:SLO-258]

Thaw in cold water only in a leak-proof package or bag, only while the water stays below 70 °F (about 21 °C), and only if the food will thaw in under 2 hours. [T:SLO-258]

## When to throw away

Food is safe while it stays frozen, but once it thaws and rises above 40 °F (about 4 °C), bacteria that were present before freezing can begin to multiply. [T:SUS-059]

During a power cut keep the freezer door closed: according to the FDA, a full freezer keeps its temperature for about 48 hours, or 24 hours if it is half full. [T:SLO-259]

When the power comes back, the FDA says that food which still contains ice crystals or is at 40 °F (about 4 °C) or below is safe to refreeze or cook; you cannot judge this by look or smell. [T:SLO-259]

JarAtlas rule (cautious): throw away a package that has no ice crystals and is above 40 °F (about 4 °C), or that you cannot check. [T:SLO-260]

The FDA also says to discard perishable food that has been at room temperature for 2 hours or more, or 1 hour if it is above 90 °F (about 32 °C) outdoors. [T:SLO-260]
